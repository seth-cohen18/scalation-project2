\section*{Overview}
This report extends the Project 1 exploratory analysis of three UCI datasets
(Auto MPG, Concrete Compressive Strength, Airfoil Self-Noise) with regression
and symbolic regression. Five techniques are evaluated using
\textbf{ScalaTion}, Python's \textbf{Statsmodels}, and \textbf{PySR}, following
the model allocation in the assignment:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Regression} (ScalaTion + Statsmodels): all 3 datasets.
    \item \textbf{Regularized Regression}, Ridge and Lasso (ScalaTion +
    Statsmodels): Auto MPG only.
    \item \textbf{Forward Feature Selection} (ScalaTion): Auto MPG and
    Concrete.
    \item \textbf{Transformed Regression}, log/sqrt/Box-Cox (ScalaTion): Auto
    MPG and Airfoil.
    \item \textbf{Symbolic Regression} (ScalaTion + PySR): all 3 datasets.
\end{enumerate}
Unless noted otherwise, models use every available predictor for a dataset
(no manual feature pre-selection), so results here are not directly comparable
to Project~1's two-feature simple regressions. Full console logs for every
ScalaTion run are in \texttt{results/<dataset>/scalation\_output.txt}; a
compact digest of coefficients and QoF is in
\texttt{results/<dataset>/scalation\_digest.txt}.

\begin{sloppypar}
\textbf{Reproducing the results.} The Python scripts in \texttt{python/} read
\texttt{data/} relative to the repository and need \texttt{statsmodels}, and
\texttt{pysr} for Section 5. To run the ScalaTion code, copy
\texttt{scala/Project2.scala} into a ScalaTion app at
\texttt{src/main/scala/project2/}, set the environment variable
\texttt{PROJECT2\_DATA\_DIR} to this repository's \texttt{data/} folder, and
run \texttt{sbt "runMain project2.project2\_autoMPG"} (likewise
\texttt{project2\_concrete} and \texttt{project2\_airfoil}).
\end{sloppypar}

\textbf{Interpretation:} weight, model\_year, and origin dominate: every
extra 1000 lbs costs about 6.5 mpg, each model year (1970--1982) is worth

counter-intuitive sign: holding the other features fixed, its coefficient is
positive ($+0.0199$ mpg per cubic inch) and significant ($p=0.008$), even though
on its own displacement is strongly negatively correlated with mpg. Cylinders'
coefficient has the expected negative sign but is not significant
($p=0.13$). Both are symptoms of multicollinearity among the engine-size
features (cylinders, displacement, horsepower and weight are all highly
correlated with each other), so their individual coefficients are not reliable
on their own.

\section{Regularized Regression (Auto MPG only)}
\begin{sloppypar}
Ridge (L2) and Lasso (L1) were fit on Auto MPG in both tools. ScalaTion's
\texttt{RidgeRegression}/\texttt{LassoRegression} auto-center $x$ and $y$

Statsmodels' \texttt{OLS.fit\_regularized} was run on standardized predictors

ScalaTion, $\alpha$ for Statsmodels) was chosen by 5-fold cross-validated

\texttt{displacement}, and \texttt{acceleration} exactly to zero, leaving a

redundant with each other once weight is in the model. ScalaTion's
Ridge/Lasso (which do not standardize predictors, so their $\lambda$ values
are on a different scale) told the same qualitative story:
\texttt{acceleration}'s coefficient shrinks the most under Lasso
($0.0806\rightarrow0.0309$), while \texttt{weight} and \texttt{model\_year} are
essentially unchanged.

\section{Forward Feature Selection}
ScalaTion's \texttt{forwardSelAll} was run on Auto MPG and Concrete (Airfoil
was left out, since it has only 5 predictors -- too few for the ordering to
be very informative -- while the other two datasets have 7--8).

\textbf{Selection criterion.} At each step ScalaTion adds the predictor that
most lowers sMAPE-IC, an information criterion equal to sMAPE plus a size
penalty $2(k+1)/m$ ($k$ predictors, $m$ rows). It is computed on ScalaTion's
training split (the first 80\% of rows), so it is \emph{not} a cross-validated
measure. For each step we also report $\bar R^2$ (training split) and the
5-fold cross-validated $R^2_{cv}$. The three criteria do not always agree on
the best subset size, so all three are shown; $k$ counts predictors and
excludes the intercept.

\textbf{Interpretation:} forward selection does shrink both models under
ScalaTion's criterion: AutoMPG from 7 to 5 predictors, and Concrete from 8 to 6.
The predictors it drops add almost nothing. After AutoMPG's third step and
Concrete's sixth, $\bar R^2$ and $R^2_{cv}$ move by less than 0.3 percentage
points, so the choice between the reduced and full models is a near-tie that
depends on the criterion. The more robust result is the \emph{order} of
selection, which acts as a model-based importance ranking. For AutoMPG,
\texttt{weight}, \texttt{model\_year} and \texttt{origin} come first, and
cross-validation says those three alone predict as well as any larger subset.
They are also three of the four predictors Lasso keeps in Section 2. The
engine-size predictors (\texttt{displacement}, \texttt{horsepower}) enter
last because they carry little information that \texttt{weight} doesn't
already. For Concrete, superplasticizer and age are selected before cement:
once workability (superplasticizer) and curing time (age) are accounted for,
cement adds less marginal predictive power than its Section 1 coefficient
alone would suggest. The two aggregates, which also had the smallest
Section 1 coefficients, are the ones dropped.

\section{Transformed Regression}
ScalaTion's \texttt{TranRegression} was run on Auto MPG and Airfoil,
applying \texttt{log(y)}, \texttt{sqrt(y)}, and Box-Cox($y$)
with $\lambda=0.4$ (the library default; Yeo-Johnson is not exposed as a
ready-made \texttt{Transform} in this ScalaTion build, so Box-Cox was used, as
permitted by the rubric). Both target variables are strictly positive, so all
three transforms are well-defined.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Effect of transforming the response on in-sample $R^2$.}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Dataset & Untransformed & log($y$) & sqrt($y$) & Box-Cox($y$), $\lambda=0.4$ \\
\midrule
Auto MPG (mpg) & 0.8215 & \textbf{0.8552} & 0.8437 & 0.8468 \\
Airfoil (dB) & \textbf{0.5157} & 0.5082 & 0.5122 & 0.5114 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Interpretation:} the two datasets tell opposite stories, which is
itself the interesting finding. \texttt{mpg} is a reciprocal-like, right-skewed
quantity (efficiency measured as distance/fuel rather than the more
physically additive fuel/distance), so every transform toward a
more-symmetric scale improves the fit; log is best, gaining $\Delta R^2
\approx 0.034$ over the untransformed model, consistent with standard
practice of regressing $\log(\text{mpg})$ or gallons-per-100-miles instead of
mpg directly. \texttt{scaled\_sound\_pressure\_level}, by contrast, is
\emph{already} a decibel (logarithmic) quantity, so applying a further
log/sqrt/Box-Cox transform does not help: it slightly worsens $R^2$, RMSE
and sMAPE on the original scale, because it fights against a scale that is already close to linear in the
underlying physics. This is a clean illustration that transformed regression
should be guided by the target's native scale, not applied by default.

\section{Symbolic Regression}
Both a power/cross-term expansion in ScalaTion and a genetic symbolic search
in PySR were run on all three datasets.

\subsection{ScalaTion}
Each dataset's predictors were expanded with $x^{0.5}$, $x^{2}$, an
intercept, and all pairwise cross terms $x_ix_j$, then \texttt{forwardSelAll}
was used to rank terms. As in Section 3, ScalaTion adds terms by sMAPE-IC on
the training split, and we also track 5-fold cross-validated $R^2_{cv}$.
Term counts below exclude the intercept. An earlier attempt that also included $x^{-1}$
and $x^{-0.5}$ terms produced a severely multicollinear, numerically unstable
design matrix (coefficients up to $10^{15}$, NaN standard errors, and an
outright crash on Concrete) since a variable's reciprocal and direct powers
are highly correlated; the power set was narrowed to $\{0.5,2\}$ to keep the
expansion well-conditioned.

\textbf{Interpretation:} the power/cross-term expansion is a real
improvement over linear regression on all three datasets, and it holds up out
of sample: cross-validated $R^2$ rises from 0.81 to 0.86 (Auto MPG), 0.60 to
0.87 (Concrete), and 0.51 to 0.63 (Airfoil). Concrete gains the most,
consistent with its strength depending on interactions between ingredients
and age.

Forward selection behaves differently on each dataset. For Auto MPG,
ScalaTion's criterion keeps 27 of 35 terms, but cross-validation peaks much
earlier: the first 11 terms ($R^2_{cv}=0.869$) predict slightly better than
either the 27-term subset or the full model (0.860). The extra terms mostly fit
noise, and a compact model is enough. For Concrete, sMAPE-IC stops at 37 of
44 terms, leaving out the last 7 added (superplasticizer\_fly\_ash,
fly\_ash\_cement, coarse\_aggregate$^{0.5}$, superplasticizer\_cement,
fine\_aggregate$^2$, age\_coarse\_aggregate, age\_cement). Cross-validation
does not support dropping them: $R^2_{cv}$ is 0.863 at 37 terms and 0.866 for
the full model. The criteria disagree here, and the honest conclusion is that
those 7 terms neither clearly help nor clearly hurt. For Airfoil, all three
criteria agree on the same 16 of 20 terms ($R^2_{cv}=0.629$, essentially
equal to the full model's 0.628). Even so, Airfoil remains the weakest of the
three datasets, pointing to nonlinear structure beyond what a low-order
power/cross-term basis captures.

ScalaTion fits coefficients for a fixed basis of up to 44 terms. PySR searches
over expression trees, so it can find operators the fixed basis doesn't
contain, such as division and logarithms.

PySR's Auto MPG equation is close to physical reasoning: it finds that fuel
economy scales with a (model-year-adjusted) constant divided by weight. It
uses only 2 fitted constants, yet reaches $R^2=0.851$, \emph{beating} the
8-parameter linear model ($R^2=0.822$), though not ScalaTion's 35-term
expansion ($R^2=0.895$). For Concrete, PySR clearly beats the linear model
($R^2=0.756$ vs.\ $0.616$) but falls well short of ScalaTion's 44-term
expansion ($R^2=0.886$). What it offers instead is a compact, readable
equation: a $\log(\text{age})\times\text{cement}/\text{water}$ interaction that
ScalaTion's $\{x^{0.5},x^2,x_ix_j\}$ basis cannot express. This lines up
with concrete science, where strength gain follows a roughly logarithmic
curing curve in age and depends on the cement/water ratio. For Airfoil,
PySR's equation ($R^2=0.556$) improves modestly on linear regression
($0.516$) but is below ScalaTion's 20-term expansion ($R^2=0.641$). The
$\texttt{frequency}\times\texttt{chord\_length}\times\texttt{thickness}$
product it finds is consistent with airfoil self-noise scaling, in which
noise depends on these geometric and aerodynamic quantities jointly. The
modest $R^2$ of every method in this section confirms that much of Airfoil's
noise variance is not captured by low-order combinations of these five
predictors.

\section*{Cross-Section Summary}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Linear regression accuracy ranks Auto MPG $>$ Concrete $>$
    Airfoil}, reflecting how close to linear each underlying physical
    process is. Once interaction terms are allowed (Section 5), Concrete
    catches up with Auto MPG (cross-validated $R^2$ 0.866 vs.\ 0.860), since
    much of its structure is in ingredient and age interactions. Airfoil
    stays last under every method.
    \item \textbf{Regularization} (Section 2) matters for interpretability
    (Lasso's sparsity confirms which of Auto MPG's correlated engine-size
    features are redundant) more than for raw accuracy, since the
    unregularized model was not overfitting to begin with.
    \item \textbf{Forward selection} (Section 3) shrank Auto MPG from 7 to 5
    predictors and Concrete from 8 to 6 under ScalaTion's sMAPE-IC
    criterion. The dropped predictors change $\bar R^2$ and $R^2_{cv}$ by
    under 0.3 percentage points. Its selection order agrees with Lasso:
    weight, model year and origin carry most of Auto MPG's signal.
    \item \textbf{Transformed regression} (Section 4) only helps when the
    transform matches the target's native scale: it clearly helped Auto MPG
    (already skewed) and did not help Airfoil (already log-scaled in dB).
    \item \textbf{Symbolic regression} (Section 5) gave the largest accuracy
    gains of any technique, and the gains hold up under cross-validation.
    ScalaTion's power/cross-term expansion was the most accurate, while
    PySR's search produced much more compact equations. Only PySR found
    terms outside a fixed polynomial basis: a $1/\text{weight}$ scaling for
    Auto MPG and a $\log(\text{age})$ curing term for Concrete. Both have
    clear physical justification, not just statistical support.
\end{itemize}
