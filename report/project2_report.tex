\section*{Overview}
This report extends the Project 1 exploratory analysis of three UCI datasets
(Auto MPG, Concrete Compressive Strength, Airfoil Self-Noise) with regression
and symbolic regression. Five techniques are evaluated using
\textbf{ScalaTion}, Python's \textbf{Statsmodels}, and \textbf{PySR}, following
the model allocation in the assignment:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Regression} (ScalaTion + Statsmodels): all 3 datasets.
    \item \textbf{Regularized Regression}, Ridge and Lasso (ScalaTion +
    Statsmodels): Auto MPG only.
    \item \textbf{Forward Feature Selection} (ScalaTion): Auto MPG and
    Concrete.
    \item \textbf{Transformed Regression}, log/sqrt/Box-Cox (ScalaTion): Auto
    MPG and Airfoil.
    \item \textbf{Symbolic Regression} (ScalaTion + PySR): all 3 datasets.
\end{enumerate}
Unless noted otherwise, models use every available predictor for a dataset
(no manual feature pre-selection), so results here are not directly comparable
to Project~1's two-feature simple regressions. Full console logs for every
ScalaTion run are in \texttt{results/<dataset>/scalation\_output.txt}; a
compact digest of coefficients and QoF is in
\texttt{results/<dataset>/scalation\_digest.txt}.

\begin{sloppypar}
\textbf{Reproducing the results.} The Python scripts in \texttt{python/} read
\texttt{data/} relative to the repository and need \texttt{statsmodels} (and
\texttt{pysr} for Section 5). To run the ScalaTion code, copy
\texttt{scala/Project2.scala} into a ScalaTion app at
\texttt{src/main/scala/project2/}, set the environment variable
\texttt{PROJECT2\_DATA\_DIR} to this repository's \texttt{data/} folder, and
run \texttt{sbt "runMain project2.project2\_autoMPG"} (likewise
\texttt{project2\_concrete} and \texttt{project2\_airfoil}).
\end{sloppypar}

features (cylinders, displacement, horsepower and weight are all highly
correlated with each other), so their individual coefficients are not reliable
on their own.

\section{Forward Feature Selection}
ScalaTion's \texttt{forwardSelAll} was run on Auto MPG and Concrete, which
have 7 and 8 predictors. Airfoil was left out because with only 5 predictors
the selection order says little.

\textbf{Selection criterion.} At each step ScalaTion adds the predictor that
most lowers sMAPE-IC, an information criterion equal to sMAPE plus a size
penalty $2(k+1)/m$ ($k$ predictors, $m$ rows). It is computed on ScalaTion's
training split (the first 80\% of rows), so it is \emph{not} a cross-validated
measure. For each step we also report $\bar R^2$ (training split) and the
5-fold cross-validated $R^2_{cv}$, because the three criteria do not always
agree on the best subset size. $k$ counts predictors and excludes the
intercept.

cement adds less marginal predictive power than its Section 1 coefficient
alone would suggest. The two aggregates are the ones dropped. They also had
the smallest Section 1 coefficients and were the only non-significant
predictors there.

\section{Transformed Regression}
ScalaTion's \texttt{TranRegression} was run on Auto MPG and Airfoil. In
every model the transformed variable is the response $y$ (\texttt{mpg} and
\texttt{scaled\_sound\_pressure\_level}); the predictors are left
untransformed. Three transforms were applied, each with ScalaTion's default
parameters:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{log:} \texttt{LogForm()}, which computes $\log(y+1)$. Its
    default shift is 1, so this is $\log(1+y)$ rather than $\log y$. Since
    mpg ranges from 9 to 46.6 and sound level from 103 to 141 dB, the shift
    barely changes the shape.
    \item \textbf{sqrt:} \texttt{RootForm()}, which computes $\sqrt{y}$.
    \item \textbf{Box-Cox:} \texttt{BoxcoxForm()}, which computes
    $(y^{\lambda}-1)/\lambda$ with $\lambda=0.4$. The rubric allows either
    Box-Cox or Yeo-Johnson. We used Box-Cox, since Yeo-Johnson is not exposed
    as a ready-made \texttt{Transform} in this ScalaTion build.
\end{itemize}
Both targets are strictly positive, so all three transforms are well-defined.
The model is fit on the transformed scale, and predictions are
back-transformed to the original units before computing $R^2$, so every
$R^2$ below is comparable to Section 1.

\textbf{Reading the coefficients:} every transform keeps the same sign as the
untransformed model for each predictor, with one exception:
\texttt{acceleration} in the Auto MPG log model, whose coefficient is tiny
and was not significant in Section 1 ($p=0.415$). The log model is the
easiest to read, because a coefficient $b$ multiplies $y+1$ by roughly
$e^{b}$. In Auto MPG, each model year raises mpg by about 2.9\%
($e^{0.0284}$), and each extra 1000 lbs lowers it by about 22\%
($e^{-0.245}$). These are percentage effects, which fit fuel economy better
than the constant per-pound effect of the linear model. In Airfoil, each
extra 1000 Hz lowers the sound level by about 1.0\%, and each extra 0.1 m of
chord by about 2.8\%.

\textbf{Interpretation:} the transforms help Auto MPG and do not help
Airfoil. \texttt{mpg} is skewed to the right and behaves like a reciprocal, since it
measures distance per unit of fuel, while fuel per unit of distance is the
quantity that adds up physically. Every transform toward a more symmetric
scale improves the fit, and log is best, gaining $\Delta R^2\approx0.034$
over the untransformed model. Regressing $\log(\text{mpg})$ or gallons per
100 miles instead of mpg is standard practice for the same reason.
\texttt{scaled\_sound\_pressure\_level} is \emph{already} measured in
decibels, a logarithmic scale, so a further log, sqrt or Box-Cox transform
slightly worsens $R^2$, RMSE and sMAPE on the original scale.

\section{Symbolic Regression}
Both a power/cross-term expansion in ScalaTion and a genetic symbolic search
in PySR were run on all three datasets.

\textbf{Physics Decomposition (AI Feynman)} & Structural breakdown via physics priors (symmetries, units) + NN fit & Slow (hours) & High & Uncovering exact analytic laws of physics from raw data \\ \addlinespace[3pt]
\textbf{Evolutionary Tree (PySR)} & Genetic mutations and crossover of operator trees & Slow to Medium (mins/hrs) & High & Open-ended scientific discovery \\ \addlinespace[3pt]
\textbf{Sparse Dynamics (SINDy)} & Sequentially thresholded ridge regression & Fast & Medium & Physical systems (ODEs/PDEs) \\ \addlinespace[3pt]
\textbf{Neural Generation (DSR)} & Token-by-token sequence generation via RL & Medium & Medium & Finding specific structural patterns \\ \addlinespace[3pt]
\textbf{Agentic Loop (SR-Scientist)} & LLM code writing, execution, and optimization & Slow (agent loops) & Very High & Noisy data needing domain knowledge \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{sloppypar}
The two tools used in this project come from different rows of this table.
ScalaTion's \texttt{SymbolicRegression} belongs to the first family: it builds
a fixed library of candidate terms ($x^{0.5}$, $x^2$, $x_ix_j$) and fits them
with deterministic least squares. It differs from FFX in how it prunes the
library: it uses greedy forward selection rather than a regularization path.
PySR is the evolutionary-tree approach. It mutates and recombines expression
trees, so it can produce forms the fixed library cannot represent, such as
quotients and logarithms.
\end{sloppypar}

Our runs show the speed and flexibility trade-off from the table. Each
dataset's full ScalaTion run, including cross-validation and forward
selection, took under 20 seconds and gave the same answer every time, but it
can only reweight the terms it was given. PySR needed one to two minutes per
dataset, plus a one-time Julia compilation, and returned compact closed-form
equations (Section~\ref{sec:pysr}) built from operators outside the library.
None of our three datasets is a time series or an ODE system, so the
time-delay and sparse-dynamics families don't apply here.

\subsection{ScalaTion}
Each dataset's predictors were expanded with $x^{0.5}$, $x^{2}$, an
intercept, and all pairwise cross terms $x_ix_j$, then \texttt{forwardSelAll}
was used to rank terms. As in Section 3, ScalaTion adds terms by sMAPE-IC on
the training split, and we also track 5-fold cross-validated $R^2_{cv}$.
Term counts below exclude the intercept. An earlier attempt that also included $x^{-1}$
and $x^{-0.5}$ terms produced a severely multicollinear, numerically unstable
design matrix (coefficients up to $10^{15}$, NaN standard errors, and an
outright crash on Concrete) since a variable's reciprocal and direct powers
are highly correlated; the power set was narrowed to $\{0.5,2\}$ to keep the
expansion well-conditioned.

\textbf{Interpretation:} the power/cross-term expansion improves on linear
regression for all three datasets, and the gain holds up out of sample: cross-validated $R^2$ rises from 0.81 to 0.86 (Auto MPG), 0.60 to

and age.

Forward selection behaves differently on each dataset. For Auto MPG,
ScalaTion's criterion keeps 27 of 35 terms, but cross-validation peaks much
earlier: the first 11 terms ($R^2_{cv}=0.869$) predict slightly better than
either the 27-term subset or the full model (0.860), so the later terms mostly
fit noise. For Concrete, sMAPE-IC stops at 37 of

fly\_ash\_cement, coarse\_aggregate$^{0.5}$, superplasticizer\_cement,
fine\_aggregate$^2$, age\_coarse\_aggregate, age\_cement). Cross-validation
does not support dropping them: $R^2_{cv}$ is 0.863 at 37 terms and 0.866 for
the full model. The two criteria disagree, and those 7 terms neither clearly
help nor clearly hurt. For Airfoil, all three criteria agree on the same 16
of 20 terms ($R^2_{cv}=0.629$, essentially equal to the full model's 0.628).
Airfoil is still the weakest of the three datasets, which suggests nonlinear
structure that a low-order power/cross-term basis can't capture.

economy scales with a (model-year-adjusted) constant divided by weight. It
uses only 2 fitted constants, yet reaches $R^2=0.851$, \emph{beating} the

product it finds is consistent with airfoil self-noise scaling, in which
noise depends on these geometric and aerodynamic quantities jointly.

\section*{Cross-Section Summary}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Accuracy by dataset}: linear regression fits Auto MPG best,
    then Concrete, then Airfoil. Once interaction terms are allowed
    (Section 5), Concrete catches up with Auto MPG (cross-validated $R^2$
    0.866 vs.\ 0.860), since much of its structure is in ingredient and age
    interactions. Airfoil stays last under every method.
    \item \textbf{Regularization} (Section 2) barely changed accuracy, since
    the unregularized Auto MPG model was not overfitting. It was more useful
    for interpretation: Lasso's sparsity shows which of the correlated
    engine-size features are redundant.
    \item \textbf{Forward selection} (Section 3) shrank Auto MPG from 7 to 5
    predictors and Concrete from 8 to 6 under ScalaTion's sMAPE-IC
    criterion. The dropped predictors change $\bar R^2$ and $R^2_{cv}$ by
    under 0.3 percentage points. Its selection order agrees with Lasso:
    weight, model year and origin carry most of Auto MPG's signal.
    \item \textbf{Transformed regression} (Section 4) helped Auto MPG, whose
    target is skewed, and did not help Airfoil, whose target is already on a
    logarithmic decibel scale.
    \item \textbf{Symbolic regression} (Section 5) gave the largest accuracy
    gains of any technique, and the gains hold up under cross-validation.
    ScalaTion's power/cross-term expansion was the most accurate, and PySR's
    equations were much more compact. Only PySR found terms outside a fixed
    polynomial basis: a $1/\text{weight}$ scaling for Auto MPG and a
    $\log(\text{age})$ curing term for Concrete, both of which match the
    physics of the problem.
\end{itemize}
